# Sex and Gender Differences in HFpEF

> Female predominance in HFpEF arises from at least seven overlapping mechanisms — sex-specific cardiac structure, vascular/arterial aging, menopausal estrogen withdrawal, amplified comorbidity risk, obstetric history, immune biology, and exercise hemodynamics — yet despite a more severe physiological phenotype, women have better cardiac-specific survival than men.

## Mechanism

Women constitute approximately 40–50% of HFpEF patients in trial cohorts and up to ~70% in some invasive-hemodynamic referral cohorts (source: [[beale2019sex]]), compared with ~25% in HFrEF (source: [[borlaug2023statement]]) — making female sex one of the strongest epidemiological discriminators between the two HF subtypes. Female HFpEF predominance arises from at least seven overlapping, non-mutually-exclusive pathways.

### 1. Sex-specific cardiac structural biology

Women's baseline cardiac structure predisposes to the HFpEF phenotype independently of comorbidities (source: [[beale2018sex]]):

| Structural feature | Women | Men | HFpEF relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| LV chamber size | Smaller | Larger | Reduced volume buffer for filling pressure |
| LV wall stress response | Concentric hypertrophy | Eccentric dilation | Concentric pattern → increased stiffness, not compensatory dilation |
| LV end-systolic elastance (Ees) | Higher | Lower | Greater resistance to filling; higher end-diastolic pressures |
| LVEF trajectory with age | Increases steeply | Stable | LVEF ≥50% threshold may underestimate relative impairment in elderly women |

When pressure-overloaded, men tend toward eccentric dilation (increased preload reserve) while women remodel concentrically (increased stiffness, reduced diastolic reserve) — the same hypertensive stimulus thus produces more diastolic dysfunction in women (source: [[beale2018sex]]). Coppi et al. corroborate this hemodynamic asymmetry, describing men's HFpEF substrate as characterised by "greater LV mass/volume, lower arterial elastance, and higher CAD incidence" — a hemodynamically distinct pattern from women's (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]).

**LVEF threshold artefact:** elderly women have higher normal LVEF than age-matched men. A woman whose age-sex-expected LVEF is 62–65% but presents with LVEF 52% may have meaningful relative systolic impairment — yet the sex-neutral ≥50% criterion classifies her as HFpEF. This raises an open question of whether female HFpEF prevalence is partly a diagnostic-threshold artefact rather than purely biological (source: [[beale2018sex]]; source: [[bozkurt2020sex]]).

### 2. Vascular and arterial aging trajectory

Women's arterial system ages differently from men's, and this vascular phenotype closely matches the HFpEF hemodynamic profile.

**Resting arterial stiffness is significantly higher in women with HFpEF** (source: [[lau2022arterialstiffness]], invasive CPET cohort, N=190): augmentation pressure ~6 mmHg higher (19.1 vs. 13.7 mmHg, P=0.003), augmentation index >10% higher (28.9% vs. 21.7%, P=0.0002), aortic pulse pressure higher (60.5 vs. 56.7 mmHg) — all remained significant after multivariable adjustment for age, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and prior MI. Ischemic predisposition (subendocardial viability ratio, SEVR) was also worse in women (125.1 vs. 147.5 mmHg, P=0.0001), driven predominantly by the systolic rather than diastolic component. Arterial *load* measures (total arterial compliance index, pulse pressure amplification) were likewise worse in women, though systemic vascular resistance index and effective arterial elastance did not differ significantly by sex.

**Arterial stiffness correlates with abnormal LV diastolic reserve more strongly in women:** a 1-SD increase in augmentation pressure was associated with 3-fold increased odds of an abnormal exercise diastolic response (ΔPCWP/ΔCO >2.0 mmHg/L/min) in women (OR 3.16, 95% CI 1.34–7.42, P=0.008) versus a non-significant 2-fold increase in men (OR 2.07, 95% CI 0.95–5.49, P=0.15); similar directionality held for augmentation index and aortic pulse pressure (source: [[lau2022arterialstiffness]]). Arterial *load* measures (as opposed to stiffness/pulsatile measures) were not associated with abnormal diastolic response in either sex.

Additional invasive hemodynamic data (source: [[beale2019sex]]): higher arterial elastance (Ea) at exercise (1.5 vs. 1.2 mmHg/mL, P<0.001), lower systemic vascular compliance (1.3 vs. 1.1 mL/mmHg, P<0.001), lower pulmonary vascular compliance (4.5 vs. 3.4 mL/mmHg, P=0.039) — consistent with reduced pulmonary vasodilatory reserve; 82% of HFpEF patients with pulmonary hypertension are female (source: [[beale2018sex]]). The Ea/Ees coupling ratio deteriorates with age in women but is maintained in men, amplifying filling-pressure elevation during any increase in cardiac demand.

These vascular differences are sex-intrinsic and manifest independently of hypertension, though hypertension substantially accelerates them (source: [[beale2018sex]]).

### 3. Menopause and estrogen withdrawal

Estrogen has direct cardioprotective effects that are lost at menopause (source: [[beale2018sex]]; mechanistic detail source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]):

**Cardiac:** estrogen promotes NO synthesis in cardiomyocytes and endothelium → ↑cGMP/PKG → titin phosphorylation → reduced passive stiffness. Withdrawal reverses this cascade: ↓NO → ↓cGMP → PKG deficiency → titin hypophosphorylation → increased cardiomyocyte stiffness, the cellular substrate of diastolic dysfunction. Coppi et al. add molecular detail: estrogen acts via ERα, ERβ, and GPR30 receptors, which normally inhibit myocardial hypertrophy and modulate intracellular calcium handling; estrogen also inhibits fibroblast proliferation, limiting myocardial fibrosis — its postmenopausal decline removes this antifibrotic brake (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]). Reduced SERCA2a (sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase) expression — the pump responsible for diastolic calcium reuptake and myofibril relaxation — is separately implicated as a general HFpEF mechanism by Coppi et al., though this review does not present SERCA2a expression itself as sex-differential; it is presented alongside, not explicitly linked to, the estrogen-receptor discussion (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]).

**Systemic/vascular:** estrogen inhibits RAAS (reduces angiotensin receptor sensitivity, reduces aldosterone secretion). Menopause-associated RAAS activation → reactive oxygen species → collagen synthesis → interstitial fibrosis → myocardial stiffness. Women also shift substrate utilisation away from fatty acid oxidation post-menopause toward less efficient metabolic pathways (source: [[beale2018sex]]).

**Net effect:** HFpEF incidence increases sharply in women after menopause, and women are expected to spend approximately 40% of their lives in a postmenopausal state in Western countries — timing that mirrors loss of estrogen-mediated cardioprotection rather than hypertensive burden accumulation alone (source: [[beale2018sex]]; source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]).

### 4. Amplified comorbidity risk

Several major HFpEF-promoting comorbidities carry disproportionately higher HF risk in women than men (source: [[beale2018sex]]):

| Comorbidity | HF risk in women | HF risk in men | Relative amplification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type 2 diabetes | 5.0× | 2.4× | 2.1× greater in women |
| Hypertension | 3.0× | 2.0× | 1.5× greater in women |
| Obesity | Higher absolute prevalence in HFpEF cohorts | Lower | — |
| Iron deficiency | More prevalent | Less prevalent | Contributes to exercise intolerance |

The diabetes amplification is mechanistically important: T2DM drives the inflammation–NO–cGMP–titin pathway more severely in women, compounds menopausal estrogen deficiency's effect on NO bioavailability, and accelerates concentric remodelling (source: [[beale2018sex]]). Coppi et al. separately note women tend toward a more "obese" cardiometabolic phenotype (higher metabolic syndrome/T2DM prevalence, worse diastolic dysfunction), while in men visceral adiposity and myocardial lipid infiltration are more mechanistically prominent — a proposed sex-differential pathway from obesity to diastolic dysfunction, though this specific mechanistic split is this review's own synthesis rather than a directly-cited primary finding (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]).

### 5. Obstetric and reproductive history

Pregnancy and its complications impose lasting cardiovascular consequences relevant to HFpEF (source: [[beale2018sex]]):

**Parity:** sustained volume overload and hemodynamic stress during each pregnancy correlates with measurable diastolic dysfunction approximately one decade after the last delivery, with higher parity associated with greater diastolic impairment.

**Pre-eclampsia:** confers a 4-fold increased risk of future HF across all HF subtypes. At 6–12 months postpartum, women with prior pre-eclampsia show persistent LV concentric remodelling, elevated LV filling pressures, and diastolic dysfunction relative to normotensive postpartum controls — an early HFpEF substrate, driven by systemic endothelial dysfunction, placental RAAS activation, and immunological vascular injury that outlasts delivery.

Coppi et al. additionally flag early-onset menopause, polycystic ovary syndrome, gestational diabetes, and recurrent pregnancy loss as under-recognised sex-specific risk factors requiring further study — this is a knowledge-gap statement from the review, not yet backed by primary data ingested in this wiki (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]).

### 6. Inflammation and immune biology

Women's immune system is inherently more proinflammatory, with direct cardiac consequences (source: [[beale2018sex]]):
- Female cardiac tissue shows sex-differentially expressed genes in NF-κB and KLF4 pathways, both regulating inflammatory cytokine production, fibrogenesis, and macrophage activation in the heart.
- Women have higher baseline high-sensitivity CRP; inflammatory markers predict HFpEF incidence more strongly in women.
- Autoimmune diseases with predominantly female prevalence (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, Sjögren's) are independently associated with diastolic dysfunction and accelerate HFpEF pathophysiology via immune-mediated myocardial inflammation and fibrosis.

### 7. Exercise hemodynamic phenotype

Direct invasive quantification of sex-hemodynamic differences during exercise (source: [[beale2019sex]], N=161, prospective invasive right-heart-catheterisation + echo):

| Parameter | Women | Men | P |
|---|---|---|---|
| PCWP per workload (mmHg/W) | 0.8 [0.5–1.2] | 0.6 [0.4–1.0] | 0.014 |
| ΔSVI (stroke volume reserve) | 8.1 mL | 19.7 mL | 0.007 |
| E/e' at exercise | 14.1 | 9.3 | <0.001 |
| Lactate per workload (mmol/L/W) | 0.09 | 0.04 | 0.003 |
| Pulmonary compliance | 4.5 mL/mmHg | 3.4 mL/mmHg | 0.039 |

Women mount greater filling-pressure elevation per unit of external work — the direct hemodynamic expression of reduced diastolic reserve, lower vascular compliance, and smaller stroke-volume reserve, and worse peripheral oxygen extraction/kinetics (source: [[beale2019sex]]).

## Sex-Specific Outcomes: A Divergent Mortality Pattern

Despite the more severe hemodynamic and vascular phenotype described above, sex differences in survival run in the *opposite* direction to what that phenotype would predict.

**Cardiac vs. non-cardiac mortality (source: [[duca2018genderdiff]], N=260, invasive hemodynamics + CMR, median follow-up 30 months):** men had significantly higher cardiac death (16.5% vs. 6.1%, P=0.008) and lower non-cardiac death (2.5% vs. 10.5%, P=0.030) than women. Male sex was an independent predictor of cardiac death in multivariable Cox regression (HR 2.639, 95% CI 1.023–6.805, P=0.045) and of the combined endpoint of cardiac death or HF hospitalisation (HR 1.588, P=0.037), but **not** of all-cause death (HR 1.164, P=0.631).

**Mode of death also differed sharply by sex:** men died almost exclusively of right heart failure (73.3% vs. 36.7% in women, P=0.020) and sudden cardiac death (13.3% vs. 0.0%, P=0.041); women more often died of infection (23.3% vs. 0.0% in men, P=0.042) (source: [[duca2018genderdiff]]).

The authors summarise this as: **men rather develop a right heart phenotype and die from HFpEF, whereas women are more likely to die with HFpEF** (source: [[duca2018genderdiff]]). Coppi et al. independently corroborate the broader pattern — women with HFpEF report lower quality of life but have superior overall survival compared with men (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]).

## Sex-Specific Diagnostic Reference Ranges

Coppi et al. compile proposed sex-specific echocardiographic and CMR thresholds for diastolic dysfunction/HFpEF (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]; the review states current major guidelines do **not** yet incorporate sex-specific cut-offs for LAVI or LA ejection fraction, despite proposed biological differences — treat the table below as a research-proposed reference set, not an endorsed guideline standard):

| Parameter | Women | Men |
|---|---|---|
| LA reservoir strain (%) | ≥35 normal, <23 abnormal | ≥33 normal, <23 abnormal |
| LAVI (mL/m²) | <34 normal, ≥34 abnormal | <34 normal, ≥34 abnormal (no sex-specific split proposed) |
| GLS (%) | ≤−20 normal, >−16 abnormal | ≤−19 normal, >−15 abnormal |
| ECV (%) | <28 normal, ≥28 abnormal | <27 normal, ≥27 abnormal |

Women exhibit slightly higher normative LA reservoir strain values than men, but the review notes pathological-reduction thresholds are still applied uniformly (<23% for both sexes) and may need refinement. Natriuretic peptide reference ranges are also sex-differential (women present with slightly higher baseline BNP/NT-proBNP) — see [[natriuretic-peptides]] for the wiki's canonical treatment of NP interpretation; this page does not duplicate that content.

## Treatment — Sex-Subgroup Trial Data (Hypothesis-Generating, Not Confirmed)

**Sacubitril/valsartan:** in PARAGON-HF, sacubitril/valsartan benefit waned with rising LVEF, but the effective LVEF ceiling for benefit was higher in women (~60%) than men (~45–50%); women were 52% of the trial and had higher LVEF and lower NT-proBNP despite worse symptoms and more obesity. NT-proBNP/cGMP responses to the drug were equivalent by sex — arguing against a simple pharmacokinetic explanation for the differential effect; a speculative estrogen–neprilysin upregulation hypothesis has been proposed but is unconfirmed (source: [[bozkurt2020sex]]).

**SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists:** Coppi et al. report that in EMPEROR-Preserved, the relative risk reduction for the primary outcome was numerically greater in women (HR 0.75) than men (HR 0.81); in DELIVER, HR 0.81 (women) vs. 0.82 (men); in SUMMIT (tirzepatide), a 38% reduction in HF events was reported in both sexes, with women representing >50% of the trial population. **The review is explicit that none of these sex-subgroup differences reached statistical significance and that sex-stratified analysis remains underpowered in all three trials** — this is a hypothesis-generating trend only, not a confirmed sex-differential treatment effect (source: [[coppi2025genderdiff]]). See [[emperor-preserved]], [[deliver]], [[summit]] for each trial's own primary results.

## Clinical Relevance

Sex is a first-order phenotyping dimension in HFpEF (see [[hfpef-phenotype-profiling]] for the broader comorbidity-phenotype framework, of which sex is one axis, not a replacement). Practical implications:
- A sex-neutral LVEF ≥50% threshold may differentially misclassify women (see LVEF-threshold artefact above).
- Women should not be assumed to have a "milder" HFpEF phenotype on the basis of better cardiac-specific survival — their hemodynamic, vascular, and exercise-physiological phenotype is measurably worse by nearly every invasive parameter studied; their outcome advantage is specific to cardiac-cause mortality, not overall symptom burden or quality of life.
- Arterial stiffness (Lau 2022) is a modifiable/targetable mechanism preferentially relevant to women — see [[arterial-stiffness]] for exercise physiology and NO-pathway detail shared across sexes.
- Sex-specific diagnostic thresholds (Coppi 2025 compilation) are not yet guideline-endorsed; use with appropriate caution pending validation.

## Open Questions

- Is female HFpEF predominance partly a diagnostic-threshold artefact (sex-neutral LVEF ≥50%) rather than purely biological?
- Does the estrogen-receptor/SERCA2a mechanism respond to hormone-replacement or estrogen-receptor-modulating therapy? No interventional estrogen trial exists in HFpEF.
- Why do women have worse hemodynamic/vascular phenotype and quality of life, yet better cardiac-specific survival? Is this a competing-risk artefact (women live long enough to die of something else) or a genuine biological protection against cardiac-specific death mechanisms (RHF, SCD)?
- Are the EMPEROR-Preserved/DELIVER/SUMMIT sex-subgroup trends (numerically favouring women, not statistically significant) real effect-modification or noise? Adequately powered sex-stratified trials do not yet exist.
- Will sex-specific echocardiographic/CMR diagnostic thresholds (Coppi 2025 compilation) be validated and adopted into major guidelines?

## Contradictions

- **Artefactual vs. biological female predominance:** the sex-neutral LVEF ≥50% threshold may classify some elderly women as HFpEF who have relative systolic impairment below their sex-age-specific normal, confounding the true biological-vs-diagnostic-threshold explanation for female HFpEF preponderance (source: [[beale2018sex]]).
- **Estrogen and sacubitril/valsartan:** estrogen may upregulate neprilysin and modulate RAAS sensitivity — a speculative mechanism for why women benefit from ARNi at higher LVEF than men — but NT-proBNP/cGMP responses to sacubitril/valsartan are equivalent by sex, making a pharmacokinetic explanation unlikely (source: [[bozkurt2020sex]]).
- **hs-TnT sex inversion:** despite women's higher HFpEF prevalence and worse hemodynamic phenotype, hs-TnT predicts outcomes more strongly in men (HR 3.33) than women (HR 1.35) — suggesting men's HFpEF is more ischemic/injury-driven while women's is more fibrotic/stiffness-driven (source: [[morfino2022biomarkers]]).
- **Worse physiology, better cardiac-specific survival (new, 2026-07-17):** women have a measurably worse hemodynamic/vascular/exercise phenotype by nearly every invasive parameter (Beale 2019, Lau 2022) yet lower cardiac-specific mortality and a different mode of death than men (Duca 2018 — men die of right heart failure/sudden cardiac death; women die with HFpEF of non-cardiac causes, particularly infection). Whether this reflects genuine sex-specific protection against cardiac-death mechanisms, a competing-risk artefact, or referral/cohort differences (Duca's cohort strictly excluded CAD and cardiac amyloidosis, both male-predominant HFpEF mimics) is unresolved.
