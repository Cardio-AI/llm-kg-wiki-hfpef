# Dynamic Hyperinflation

> Exercise-induced increase in end-expiratory lung volume, present in the majority of obese HFpEF patients and increasing in prevalence from submaximal to peak exercise; distinct from expiratory flow limitation itself, and the specific mechanical driver of externally elevated measured PCWP and of the ventilatory exercise ceiling in obese HFpEF.

## Aliases

| Alias | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| DH | abbreviation | standard shorthand in the exercise-physiology literature |
| ΔEELV | descriptive | the quantitative measure used to define dynamic hyperinflation (change in end-expiratory lung volume from rest) |

## Definition and Measurement

Dynamic hyperinflation (DH) is defined operationally as an increase in end-expiratory lung volume (EELV) of **≥150 mL from rest** during exercise. It is distinct from — though frequently co-occurring with — expiratory flow limitation (EFL), a separate phenomenon describing constrained expiratory airflow. In the Leahy 2025 cohort, EFL severity (percentage of tidal volume affected) did **not** correlate with pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), whereas the magnitude of DH did — establishing DH, not EFL, as the hemodynamically relevant mechanical phenomenon. (source: Leahy2025HeartLung)

## Prevalence and Patient Characteristics

In 55 obese HFpEF patients (NCT04068844; mean age 71±7y, 64% women, BMI 38±7 kg/m²):

| Timepoint | Prevalence of DH |
|---|---|
| 20W exercise | 62% (n=34) |
| Peak exercise | 85% (n=47) |

Expiratory flow limitation was present in 75% of patients at 20W and 87% at peak exercise. (source: Leahy2025HeartLung)

Patients who developed DH had a higher BMI (median 39 vs. 33 kg/m²; P=0.013), lower FVC, FEV₁, TLC, and FEF₂₅₋₇₅ (reduced baseline pulmonary function), and shorter height, compared with patients without DH — but did not differ in age, NT-proBNP, or LVEF. This indicates DH is driven by obesity-related ventilatory mechanics rather than by HFpEF severity per se. (source: Leahy2025HeartLung)

## Mechanism

Excess abdominal and chest-wall mass in obesity produces expiratory flow limitation, which forces an increase in EELV during exercise as tidal volume expansion becomes constrained (EILV approaches total lung capacity, limiting further tidal volume increase; ventilation can then only increase via increased respiratory rate, raising the dead-space ratio and worsening ventilatory efficiency). (source: Leahy2025HeartLung; source: Babb2026VentilatoryLimit)

The resulting hyperinflated lung state increases intrathoracic pressure and externally compresses the pulmonary vasculature. Because PCWP is measured relative to atmospheric pressure in clinical catheterisation, this elevated intrathoracic pressure raises the **measured** PCWP independent of any change in true LV transmural filling pressure. The magnitude of hyperinflation (ΔEELV) correlated with the magnitude of PCWP rise: r²=0.112 (P=0.013) at 20W and r²=0.167 (P=0.002) at peak exercise, and also correlated with changes in mean PA pressure and transpulmonary pressure. (source: Leahy2025HeartLung)

### Quantitative PCWP Association

| Timepoint | DH Group | No-DH ("Typical Inflation") Group | P |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rest | 9±4 mmHg | 7±4 mmHg | NS |
| 20W exercise | 23±8 mmHg | 16±6 mmHg | 0.005 |

## Relationship to Exercise Capacity

A companion randomised crossover study (Babb 2026, N=42 obese HFpEF; nitroglycerin vs. placebo) tested whether reducing PCWP pharmacologically could relieve the exercise limitation associated with DH. Nitroglycerin significantly reduced PCWP at rest and at 20W (both P<0.01) — confirming effective cardiac unloading — but had **no effect on exercise capacity** (peak work rate unchanged) and **no effect on breathing mechanics** (EELV, EILV, and FVC unchanged before vs. after NTG; R²=0.96 for EELV). Patients stopped exercising at the same ventilatory point regardless of cardiac unloading. (source: Babb2026VentilatoryLimit)

This establishes that the DH-driven elevation of measured PCWP is **mechanically unidirectional**: dynamic hyperinflation raises measured PCWP by compressing the cardiovascular system, but reducing PCWP cannot, in turn, relieve the ventilatory constraint that produced the hyperinflation in the first place. See [[heart-lung-interactions]] for the full PCWP-elevation mechanism and [[ventilatory-limitation]] for the exercise-ceiling consequence.

## Clinical and Diagnostic Implications

- Exercise right-heart catheterisation — the gold-standard diagnostic test for HFpEF — may **overestimate true LV filling pressure** in obese patients who develop dynamic hyperinflation, because measured (atmospheric-referenced) PCWP includes a mechanical contribution from elevated intrathoracic pressure that is not present in patients with typical (non-hyperinflating) ventilatory responses.
- Because DH — not EFL — is the parameter that correlates with PCWP, assessment of exercise EELV change, rather than flow-limitation severity alone, may be needed to interpret exercise hemodynamic testing correctly in obese HFpEF.
- Interventions that reduce chest-wall/abdominal mass (e.g., weight loss) would be expected to reduce DH and its associated PCWP elevation, whereas interventions that reduce PCWP alone (nitrates, diuretics) would not be expected to relieve the ventilatory constraint or improve exercise capacity in patients whose limitation is primarily ventilatory. (source: Leahy2025HeartLung; source: Babb2026VentilatoryLimit)

## Open Questions

- Would correction for pleural (transmural) pressure during exercise catheterisation meaningfully change HFpEF diagnostic classification in obese patients with dynamic hyperinflation?
- Does weight loss (lifestyle or pharmacological, e.g. GLP-1 receptor agonists) reduce dynamic hyperinflation prevalence and magnitude, and does this translate into measurable gains in exercise capacity independent of cardiac effects?
- Why does EFL severity fail to correlate with PCWP when DH does, given that EFL is described as a contributor to DH — is the relationship between the two nonlinear, or do they reflect partially independent ventilatory mechanisms?
