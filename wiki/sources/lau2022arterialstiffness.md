# Lau 2022 — Sex Differences in Arterial Stiffness in HFpEF

> Invasive CPET cohort (N=190; 107 women, 83 men): women with HFpEF have significantly greater arterial stiffness (augmentation pressure ~6 mmHg higher, augmentation index >10% higher, both P<0.0001) and worse arterial load than men despite similar blood pressures; arterial stiffness is associated with abnormal exercise diastolic reserve (ΔPCWP/ΔCO >2.0 mmHg·L⁻¹·min) more strongly in women (OR 3.16) than men (OR 2.07).

## Key Findings

- **Resting arterial stiffness higher in women:** AP 19.1 mmHg (women) vs. 13.7 mmHg (men), P=0.003; AIx 28.9% (women) vs. 21.7% (men), P=0.0002. After multivariable adjustment (age, BMI, HTN, diabetes, smoking, prior MI): AP β=0.72 SD higher in women (P<0.0001), AIx β=0.67 SD higher (P<0.001), AoPP β=0.5 SD higher (P<0.0001).
- **Worse ischemic predisposition in women:** SEVR 125.1 mmHg (women) vs. 147.5 mmHg (men), P=0.0001 — driven predominantly by the systolic (TTI) rather than diastolic (DPTI) component.
- **Worse arterial load in women:** lower TACI (β=−0.44 SD, P<0.0001) and lower PPA (β=−0.50 SD, P<0.0001); no significant sex difference in SVRi or Eai (P>0.05 both).
- **Arterial stiffness correlates with abnormal diastolic reserve, more strongly in women:** age-adjusted odds of abnormal ΔPCWP/ΔCO (>2.0 mmHg/L/min) per 1-SD increase — AP: OR 3.16 (95% CI 1.34–7.42, P=0.008) in women vs. OR 2.07 (95% CI 0.95–5.49, P=0.15) in men; AIx: OR 2.11 (95% CI 1.03–4.36, P=0.04) in women vs. OR 1.93 (95% CI 0.78–4.77, P=0.16) in men; AoPP: OR 2.96 (95% CI 1.25–7.00, P=0.01) in women vs. OR 1.86 (95% CI 0.78–4.41, P=0.16) in men. SEVR (worse ischemic predisposition) also associated with worse diastolic response in women (OR 0.41, P=0.03) but not significantly in men (OR 0.66, P=0.24).
- **Arterial load measures (SVRi, Eai, PPA) were not associated with abnormal diastolic response** in either sex — only the pulsatile/stiffness measures (AP, AIx, AoPP, SEVR) showed the association.
- **BMI/weight inversely correlated with arterial stiffness, more strongly in women** (AP: r=−0.32, P=0.0009 in women vs. r=−0.23, P=0.04 in men) — authors note this runs counter to general-population data linking obesity to increased arterial stiffness, and suggest possible lead-time bias (an "obesity paradox" analogue).

## Methods (brief)

Invasive radial arterial pressure tracings (SphygmoCor, generalized transfer function) obtained simultaneously with pulmonary artery catheter hemodynamics during upright cycle ergometry CPET. Multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, BMI, HTN, diabetes, smoking status, prior MI; SphygmoCor-derived measures additionally adjusted for heart rate. Sensitivity analyses reclassifying HFpEF using alternative criteria (rPCWP ≥15, or ΔPCWP/ΔCO >2.0 with peak exPCWP ≥15 and % predicted peak VO₂ <80%) showed similar, attenuated findings.

## Limitations

Referral-based sample (clinically indicated invasive CPET) may be subject to referral bias. Observational — causality cannot be inferred. Height differences between men and women may partly explain arterial-stiffness differences (SEVR association with sex was attenuated after adjusting for height/weight instead of BMI). Single/dual-centre exercise-referral HFpEF phenotype may reflect earlier disease stage and may not generalise to HFpEF samples with resting congestion.
